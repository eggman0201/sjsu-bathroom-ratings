                              Can anyone see me down here? 
                              









                                                                                                                         Helloooo…? 



















                Oh, you can? But this text is tiny and a pain in the ass to read? And some of you probably can't see me without your glasses?

That tiny text was probably, while still legible for many of you, still difficult to read. Now, for those blessed with 20/20 eyesight, imagine if you navigated the whole world with text that was always a little too small for you to read while no one else had an issue with it, and every time you brought up how the text could stand to be a bit bigger, people tried to convince you you were overreacting. That is, in essence, the struggle disabled people face even in ADA-compliant bathrooms.

For context, I am able-bodied at the time of writing this. The last time I needed a mobility aid was over a decade ago when I sprained my ankle, so I don't usually think about things like accessibility in bathrooms. However, I also care about people, and I've known for a while that many bathrooms, even if they are ADA (or whatever equivalent they have in their country, if they even have one) compliant, there is always more an institution could do to make life more dignified for disabled people.

The ADA guidelines were updated in 2025, but the last update regarding bathrooms, at least to my knowledge, was in 2010. Certainly, these guidelines can't exactly be updated every year, but we should be reevaluating spaces and seeing how we can make things more accessible for disabled people. I think that ADA guidelines should serve as a bare minimum, but institutions, if it's possible for them to do so, should aim to include as many amenities as possible for disabled people.

This is where that emergency call “button” comes in. I wrote “button” for all of my bathroom reviews, but they also often come in the form of cords. These systems are put in place in case someone in the stalls falls and needs help getting back up, or if there's an evacuation situation and there's someone who needs personnel to help them evacuate. They are not required by the ADA, but they're definitely one of those features that you don't really think you need until you need them.

My point in highlighting the buttons in particular is to draw attention to the fact that if a disabled person has some sort of emergency where they need help, they do not have the means to do so easily. For those who don't need mobility aids, imagine if you somehow managed to get a nasty charlie horse that won't go away in a bathroom. You're writhing on the ground in pain, and you just can't get up. Without these systems, you're essentially left there to writhe until some poor random person stumbles across you. Physically disabled people are particularly vulnerable to emergencies where they will need someone to help them get up and assess if they need further medical attention, so why not have a system in place to do exactly that?

I think a lot of able-bodied people take their able-bodied-ness for granted, so they think things like this are overkill. First of all, most people either die young or become disabled, so odds are, you will be disabled if you aren't already. Second of all, disabled people–in particular, mobility aid users–have no choice but to worry about having these aids and features because… duh.

Overall, my point is just that we can stand to make the world a more dignified place for everyone to live in. Throughout this project, I've come to realize that accessibility is not a one-and-done deal but an ongoing conversation with the community towards best practice. I don't fault SJSU for not being perfect all the time; it's actually pretty decent in terms of accessibility, but we can always aim higher.
